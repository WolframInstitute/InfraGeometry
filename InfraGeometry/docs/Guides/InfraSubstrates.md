## Abstract

*Abstract written by Pavel Hajek on 27 October 2026.*

We consider discrete substrates given by a **graph** describing connectivity between vertices. A hypothetical **infra geometer** walks on it to discover its geometry. More generally, we can imagine a **computational observer** with bounded computation time and memory, and ask how it perceives the graph. In the Wolfram Physics Project, the **spatial graph** has vertices corresponding to events in a **simultaneity slice** of a causal graph for a chosen observer. Spatial connectivity comes from the lowest common ancestor relation, connecting vertices just one light tick away, while persistence across time comes from forward causal relations.

The geometric perception of a substrate depends on the observer. As a minimal model of this dependence, we introduce a scalar **observer scale**: the smallest meaningful size, at which the observer aggregates or measures using balls of that scale. This lets us study **renormalization of the geometry**.

For our purposes, we distinguish several types of substrates. **Open substrates** represent infinitesimal neighborhoods of their centers, with the observer scale set to the radius. As the scale and the graph grow, we have more rays from the center, viewed as germs of paths. We study the emergence of a **linear structure** using midpoints, a **metric** using shortest path projection, and a **Riemann tensor** using volume measurements around the center. We can cut out these patches as balls, for example from plane or hyperbolic tessellations, and remove rim edges to indicate potential unboundedness.

Following Riemann, we think of space as a **webbing of paths**: continuous in his case and discrete in ours. He introduced the **Riemannian metric** as the simplest infinitesimal quantity producing a length element with natural properties. In continuous Riemannian geometry, the metric is extra data on a smooth structure. In infrageometry, all geometric information is already contained in the graph, so defining continuous geometry through **Gromov-Hausdorff limits** of sequences of graphs is very natural.

For **closed substrates**, we define natural sequences of **tessellations of closed surfaces** that could converge to continuous Riemannian geometries. The tessellations include regular and uniform maps such as flat tori and hyperbolic quotients. Their **combinatorial curvature, Euler characteristic and genus** describe the surface structure.

Going in the other direction, we discretize continuous geometry using **uniform-length graphs**: contact graphs of hard-sphere packings with nearly equal edge lengths. These graphs capture geometry directly in their combinatorial structure, since no edge labels are needed to keep track of lengths. We also use **barycentric subdivision** to study convergence.

We also consider the **1-skeletons of hypergraphs** produced by rewriting in the **Wolfram Model**. These differ from the spatial graphs above. We eventually want to ask whether every Riemannian manifold is a **Gromov-Hausdorff limit** of a graph sequence generated by a finite collection of rewriting rules, and how its geometry is determined by properties of those rules.

We also consider **exotic substrates** such as trees, complete graphs and fractal graphs. Any substrate can be **inflated** by growing a fiber over every vertex; **fibered substrates** form a corresponding catalogue of fibrations.

More concretely, the named example substrates come in **three sizes**: `"Small"`, `"Medium"` and `"Large"`.
<code>[InfraSubstrate]()[*name*, *size*]</code> gives the graph, <code>[InfraSubstrateStyle]()[*name*, *size*]</code> gives its drawing options, and <code>[InfraSubstrateCode]()[*name*, *size*]</code> gives the held code that builds it.

## Functions

### Named substrates

- `InfraSubstrate` the named example substrate at size "Small", "Medium" or "Large"
- `InfraSubstrateStyle` the options a substrate is drawn with at a given size
- `InfraSubstrateCode` the held code that builds a named substrate

### Tessellation graphs

- `TessellationGraph` the smallest regular map of type $\{p, q\}$, or a uniform map, as a graph
- `TorusTessellation` the flat torus with the square, triangular or hexagonal tessellation
- `TessellationNeighborhoodGraph` the $r$-ball cut from the infinite $\{p, q\}$ tessellation
- `GridGraph` (WL), `TorusGraph` (WL), `PolyhedronData` (WL), `GraphData` (WL)

### Surface-like graphs

- `TessellationCurvature` the combinatorial Gaussian curvature at a vertex of a map
- `TessellationEulerCharacteristic` the Euler characteristic $V - E + F$ of a tessellation graph
- `TessellationGenus` the genus of the surface a map lies on

### Uniform-length graphs

- `UniformLengthGraph` the contact graph of a hard-sphere packing of a region
- `UniformLengthEmbedding` coordinates under which every edge has length close to 1
- `GraphEmbedding` (WL) the coordinates a graph is drawn with

### Fractals and trees

- `SierpinskiGraph` the trivalent Sierpinski graph
- `BetheGraph` the finite Bethe lattice of $n$ shells and coordination number $z$
- `BranchingSequenceTree` the spherically symmetric tree whose branching depends only on depth
- `KaryTree` (WL), `CompleteKaryTree` (WL)

### Preparing a substrate

- `CenterGraph` the substrate cut to the ball about its centre
- `BoundarylessGraph` the substrate with its rim edges removed
- `GraphExteriorBoundary` the rim vertices, whose neighbourhood is not closed

### Inflated substrates

- `InflateGraph` a fiber of new vertices grown over every vertex, with random vertical and horizontal edges
- `InflatedVertex` the $i$-th fiber vertex over a base vertex of an inflated graph

### Fibered substrates

- `InfraFiberedSubstrate` the named example fibrations: products, coverings, tangent and displacement bundles, branched fibrations
